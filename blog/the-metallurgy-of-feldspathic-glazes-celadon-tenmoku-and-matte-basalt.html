1. The Chemical Anatomy of High-Fire Ceramic Glazes

A ceramic glaze is a specially formulated, vitreous coating of glass that is fused onto a ceramic clay body through high-temperature kiln firing. Far from being a cosmetic paint layer, a high-fire feldspathic glaze forms an integral chemical bond with the underlying porcelain, creating an impermeable barrier against moisture, stains, and bacteria while imparting magnificent tactile depth, color refraction, and gloss.

In haute cuisine, glaze formulation requires a meticulous balance of materials science and artistic intuition. Glazes must interact harmoniously with cutlery, withstand harsh automated dishwashing detergents, resist acidic fruit and citrus vinaigrettes without chemical leaching, and provide a visually compelling background that complements culinary presentations.

2. The Unity Formula: Silica Network Formers, Alumina & Fluxes

Every ceramic glaze is engineered using the Seger Unity Formula, which classifies glaze raw materials into three functional oxide categories:

  • Glass Formers ($RO_2$, primarily $SiO_2$): Silica provides the core tetrahedral glass network that hardens upon cooling.
  • Refractory Stabilizers ($R_2O_3$, primarily $Al_2O_3$): Alumina acts as a structural stabilizer, increasing the viscosity of the molten glass in the kiln so it does not slide off vertical plate rims.
  • Fluxes ($RO / R_2O$, such as $CaO, K_2O, Na_2O, MgO$): Fluxes lower the exceptionally high melting point of pure silica ($1,710^\circ\text{C}$) down to achievable kiln vitrification temperatures ($1,250^\circ\text{C}\text{--}1,300^\circ\text{C}$).

3. Pale Jade Celadon: Iron Oxide Reduction & Wood-Ash Chemistry

Celadon represents one of the most revered and technically demanding glaze families in ceramic history. Originally developed in Song Dynasty China to emulate the luminous green-blue hue of precious jade stone, true celadon is achieved using a feldspathic base containing 0.8% to 1.5% red iron oxide ($Fe_2O_3$) and washed natural wood ash.

During the reduction kiln firing at 1,280°C, oxygen-deficient combustion gases pull oxygen molecules from the ferric oxide ($Fe_2O_3$), converting it into ferrous oxide ($FeO$). When dissolved in the molten silica-calcium flux matrix, ferrous oxide reflects delicate shades of sea-foam green, pale olive, and sky blue. The natural wood ash provides trace phosphorus and titania, producing micro-bubbles that scatter light to create a soft, ethereal depth.

4. Tenmoku Glazes: Iron Saturation & Oil-Spot Micro-Nucleation

Tenmoku (historically celebrated in Japanese tea ceremonies and Song Dynasty Jian ware) is an iron-supersaturated glaze containing 6% to 10% iron oxide in a high-calcium feldspathic base. At peak kiln temperatures (1,280°C), the iron completely dissolves into the fluid molten glass.

As the kiln cools slowly, the iron exceeds its solubility limit in the glass matrix, triggering micro-crystallization. Iron crystals nucleate and float to the surface, creating stunning "oil-spot," "hare's fur," or metallic rust patterns across a deep, inky-black glass ground. Tenmoku plates provide dramatic contrast for pale white culinary elements, such as poached turbot, fresh scallops, and white asparagus.

5. Matte Basalt Finishes: Wollastonite & Micro-Crystalline Satin Textures

Modern fine dining has witnessed a surging appreciation for satin-matte dinnerware that eliminates harsh overhead spotlight reflections in restaurant dining rooms. Achieving a true satin matte requires avoiding under-fired, rough glazes that trap food grease and knife marks.

TableAndPlate utilizes high-temperature crystalline matte formulations rich in wollastonite (calcium silicate, $CaSiO_3$) and magnesium. As the glaze cools from 1,280°C, millions of microscopic, sub-visual calcium-magnesium silicate crystals precipitate across the surface. These micro-crystals diffuse incident light rays in all directions to create a silky, tactile satin finish while preserving a completely vitrified, sealed glass barrier beneath.

6. Non-Toxic Lead-Free Formulation & Acid Leach Resistance

Historical pottery glazes frequently relied on low-melting lead and cadmium compounds to achieve bright colors and high gloss. In commercial gastronomy, lead and cadmium present grave health risks because acidic foods (citrus juices, vinegars, culinary berry reductions) can chemically leach toxic heavy metals from the glaze matrix into the food.

TableAndPlate operates a strictly certified 100% lead-free, cadmium-free, and barium-free ceramic atelier. All our glazes are formulated from natural non-toxic earth minerals—kaolin, quartz, potash feldspar, calcium carbonate, dolomite, and food-grade iron and cobalt oxides. Every production batch undergoes rigorous 24-hour 4% acetic acid leach testing under ASTM C738 and California Proposition 65 protocols, consistently registering 0.00 ppm detectable leaching.

7. Thermal Expansion Coefficient Matching: Eliminating Crazing

Crazing is a network of fine hairline cracks that appear in a glaze when the glaze contracts more than the underlying clay body during cooling from the kiln. In restaurant dinnerware, crazing is unacceptable because bacteria, oils, and wash water can seep through the cracks into the clay body, creating foul odors and structural weakness.

To permanently eliminate crazing, TableAndPlate engineers the coefficient of thermal expansion ($\alpha$) of our glazes to match or sit slightly below that of the porcelain clay body ($\alpha_{\text{body}} \approx 6.0\times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$, $\alpha_{\text{glaze}} \approx 5.6\times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$). This keeps the glaze under mild permanent compression, preventing crack propagation even when transitioning from 70°C plate warmers into cold service.

8. Cutlery Marking Resistance: Zirconium & Quartz Hardness

Cutlery marking (grey pencil-like lines on plate surfaces) is not a scratch in the glaze; rather, it occurs when metal from stainless steel cutlery is rubbed off and deposited onto a rough or abrasive matte glaze surface.

To eliminate cutlery marking, TableAndPlate introduces zirconium silicate ($ZrSiO_4$) and finely ground quartz crystals into our glaze unity formulas. Zirconium raises the Mohs surface hardness of the fired glaze to 7.5 (significantly harder than 18/10 stainless steel at 5.5 to 6.0), creating a glass-smooth microscopic topography where cutlery glides effortlessly without abrading.

9. Commercial Dishwasher Alkali Etch Resistance

Commercial hospitality dishwashers utilize high-alkalinity caustic detergents (pH 11.5 to 13.0) and high-pressure water jets at 85°C to sterilize tableware. Over hundreds of cycles, low-quality glazes lose their gloss and turn cloudy due to alkaline hydrolysis of the silica network.

Our high-fire feldspathic glazes incorporate alumina ($Al_2O_3 \ge 0.40\text{ moles}$ in unity formula) and zinc oxide, which dramatically enhances alkaline chemical resistance. Tested across 3,000 simulated commercial wash cycles, our plates maintain 100% specular gloss retention and surface brilliance.
